\section{Background MARL}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter establishes the theoretical foundations required to contextualize the neuro-symbolic multi-agent architectures presented in this thesis.
We begin by formalizing single-agent Markovian decision processes and policy optimization. We then extend these concepts to multi-agent environments under partial observability, detailing the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm and Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO). Finally, we examine temporal abstraction frameworks—specifically Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) and the Options framework—before reviewing modern integrations of Large Language Models (LLMs) as cognitive controllers.

\subsection{Fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:rl_fundamentals}

Reinforcement learning (RL) formalizes sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where an autonomous agent learns to optimize its behavior through interaction with a dynamic environment\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}. Imagine an agent dropped into a complex grid-world, tasked with navigating a forest to collect wood. At each step, it observes its surroundings, chooses a direction, and receives a numerical reward if it successfully harvests the resource.

\subsubsection{Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)}
\label{subsec:mdp}

A fully observable single-agent control problem is mathematically formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)~\parencite{Szepesvári_2011}.
Conceptually, an MDP provides a structured framework for modeling decision-making in environments where outcomes are partly random and partly under the control of the agent. 

Figure~\ref{fig:mdp_loop} illustrates this foundational interaction loop. At each discrete timestep, the agent observes the current state of the world, consults its internal strategy (the policy $\pi$) to select an action, and executes it. The environment then computes the consequences, responding with the subsequent state and a scalar reward signal.

To solve this grid-world mathematically, the system relies on the \emph{Markov Property}. This property asserts a very simple rule: the future depends strictly on the present, not the past. If the agent knows its current state (where it is right now), memorizing the entire path it took to get there provides no additional advantage. Formally, the transition to the next state is independent of historical trajectories: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}[S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t = s_t, A_t = a_t, S_{t-1} = s_{t-1}, \dots] = \mathbb{P}[S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t = s, A_t = a]
\end{equation}

Building on this property, the MDP is formally defined by the 5-tuple $\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$~\parencite{Szepesvári_2011}.
We can map each of these mathematical variables directly to our wood-collecting agent:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of all valid states. In our example, a state $s$ includes the agent's $(x, y)$ coordinates on the grid and whether its inventory currently contains wood.
    \item $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of available actions. For our agent, this might be limited to \{Move North, Move South, Interact/Chop\}.
    \item $\mathcal{P}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{S} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is the transition probability. If the agent chooses the 'Move North' action, $\mathcal{P}$ defines the chance it actually lands on the tile above (e.g., 100\%, or perhaps 90\% if the environment includes slippery terrain).
    \item $\mathcal{R}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the reward function. This is the environment's feedback, such as emitting $+1.0$ for successfully chopping the wood, or $-0.01$ for wasting a step.
    \item $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the discount factor. This is a mathematical trick (e.g., 0.99) that makes immediate rewards slightly more valuable than future rewards, forcing the agent to collect the wood efficiently rather than wandering the grid forever.
\end{itemize}

To navigate this world, the agent needs a strategy, known as a \emph{policy}. Rather than a rigid set of if/then rules, modern RL utilizes a stochastic (probabilistic) policy, denoted mathematically as $\pi(a \mid s) = \mathbb{P}[A_t = a \mid S_t = s]$~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}.
Intuitively, this means that given a specific state $s$, the policy outputs a probability distribution over all possible actions. For instance, if the agent is standing directly next to a tree, its policy $\pi$ might assign a 95\% probability to the 'Chop' action, and a 5\% probability to 'Move South' to encourage a slight amount of random exploration.

The ultimate goal of the agent is to optimize this policy to maximize its \emph{expected discounted return}, denoted as $G_t$~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}.
While the notation $G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1}$ may appear intimidating, it simply represents the agent's "total lifetime score."
It is the sum of every reward the agent expects to collect from the current moment $t$ until the end of the episode, with future rewards multiplied by the discount factor $\gamma$ so they carry slightly less weight than immediate victories.

\subsubsection{Policy Optimization and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}
\label{subsec:ppo}

To train our wood-collecting agent, we must optimize its policy. Instead of memorizing a rigid table of every possible grid coordinate, modern reinforcement learning parameterizes the policy using a neural network, denoted as $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ with trainable weights $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}. This network is commonly referred to as the \emph{Actor}.

To help the Actor learn, we introduce a second neural network called the \emph{Critic}, parameterized by weights $\phi$. The Critic learns the \emph{Value function}, $V_\phi(s)$, which is its estimate of the total expected return from a given state. Conceptually, this is the agent's "gut feeling" about a location. If the agent stands next to a forest, the Critic outputs a high value; if it stands next to a hazard, it outputs a low value. 

\subsubsection{The Temporal Difference (TD) Error}
\label{subsec:td_error}

Because the Critic's Value function is a prediction, it requires a mathematical mechanism to measure when its predictions are wrong. This is achieved via the \emph{Temporal Difference Error} (TD-Error), denoted as $\delta_t$~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}. 

To build intuition, consider our lumberjack agent. Suppose the Critic predicts that from its current state $s_t$, it expects to gather 50 future points ($V_\phi(s_t) = 50$). The agent then executes an action, immediately receives a small reward of $5$ from the environment ($r_t = 5$), and lands in a new state $s_{t+1}$. The Critic evaluates this new state and predicts 45 future points ($V_\phi(s_{t+1}) = 45$). 

The total updated estimate is the immediate reward plus the discounted future prediction: $r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$. If we assume $\gamma=1$ for simplicity, the new estimate is $5 + 45 = 50$. Because the new estimate perfectly matches the old prediction ($50 = 50$), the TD-Error is exactly 0. The Critic was perfectly accurate. 

However, if the agent unexpectedly finds a hidden cache of wood and receives an immediate reward of $20$, the new estimate becomes $20 + 45 = 65$. The TD-Error is the gap between reality and expectation: $\delta_t = 65 - 50 = +15$. This positive "surprise" signal forces the Critic to update its weights to make better predictions in the future. Formally, the TD-Error is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td_error}
    \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)
\end{equation}

Building upon this concept, when the agent takes an action, we calculate the \emph{Advantage}, denoted as $A(s, a)$. Modern Actor-Critic algorithms typically estimate this Advantage using a discounted sum of TD-Errors (Generalized Advantage Estimation). Ultimately, the Advantage directly utilizes this "surprise factor" to measure how much better (or worse) the chosen action was compared to what the Critic originally expected.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:actor_critic}, this architecture creates a continuous feedback loop. The Critic computes the Advantage to evaluate the Actor's choice, and this signal dictates the direction of the Actor's policy gradient update ($\nabla_\theta$), pushing it to favor successful actions in the future. Simultaneously, the Critic updates its own weights ($\nabla_\phi$) to improve its future predictions.

Mathematically, the objective is to maximize the performance function $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[G_0]$ via gradient ascent. (In this standard notation, the subscript $\theta$ refers to the network's trainable weights, while a superscript, as in $Q^{\pi_\theta}$, denotes an evaluation assuming the agent strictly follows that policy). 

By the Policy Gradient Theorem~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}, this update direction is analytically expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho^{\pi_\theta}, a \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) A^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) \right]
\end{equation}
where the Advantage function is formally defined as the difference between the action-value and the state-value: $A^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) = Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) - V^{\pi_\theta}(s)$~\parencite{Sutton_Barto_2018}. 

To understand this intuitively, $V^{\pi_\theta}(s)$ evaluates how good it is to simply \emph{be} in a state, representing the baseline expectation. Conversely, the action-value $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)$ evaluates the return of taking a \emph{specific action} in that state before resuming the regular policy. Therefore, subtracting $V$ from $Q$ isolates the unique Advantage of that specific action over the baseline.

While this formulation is mathematically sound, standard policy gradient estimation is highly fragile. If our agent randomly executes a perfect sequence of actions and finds a massive cache of wood, the calculated Advantage will be enormous. Standard gradient ascent would react by drastically altering the network's weights to favor that exact sequence again. This aggressive update frequently causes the network to "fall off a cliff," catastrophically forgetting previously learned fundamental rules (such as avoiding boundaries).

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) resolves this fragility by enforcing a strict mathematical guardrail, known as a clipped surrogate objective~\parencite{Schulman_Wolski_Dhariwal_Radford_Klimov_2017}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppo_clip}
    L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min\left( r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \; \text{clip}\bigl(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\bigr)\hat{A}_t \right) \right]
\end{equation}

To understand this equation efficiently, we can break it into two parts. First, $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$ is the probability ratio: if it is greater than 1, the action is more likely to be chosen now than it was before the update. Second, the $\epsilon$ hyperparameter (typically $0.2$, as established by \textcite{Schulman_Wolski_Dhariwal_Radford_Klimov_2017}) acts as a hard speed limit. 

If an action yields a massive positive Advantage, the network will naturally try to increase the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ exponentially. However, the $\text{clip}$ function truncates this ratio at $1 + \epsilon$ (i.e., $1.2$). The $\min$ operator then forces the optimizer to use this truncated version, completely ignoring any further gradient once the probability of that action has increased by 20\%. By mathematically ignoring overly enthusiastic updates, PPO ensures the agent learns smoothly and stably without destructive variance spikes.

\subsubsection{Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence and Early Stopping}
\label{subsec:kl_divergence}

While the clipping mechanism described in Equation~\ref{eq:ppo_clip} acts as a per-action speed limit, PPO implementations frequently employ an additional, broader safety net known as \emph{early stopping} based on Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence~\parencite{Schulman_Wolski_Dhariwal_Radford_Klimov_2017}. 

KL divergence is a fundamental concept in information theory and statistics that measures how much one probability distribution diverges from a second, reference probability distribution. In the context of reinforcement learning, we use it to measure the ``distance'' between the agent's new updated policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}$ and its old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ before the current optimization step began. For discrete action spaces, the KL divergence is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl_div}
    D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \parallel \pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a \mid s) \log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}(a \mid s)}\right)
\end{equation}
Importantly, KL divergence is asymmetric (i.e., $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \neq D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P)$), meaning it specifically measures the information lost when the new policy is used to approximate the old policy's distribution. It is always non-negative and equals exactly zero only when the two distributions are identical.

To build intuition, consider an agent standing next to a tree. Under the old policy, it might have a 90\% probability of choosing to `Chop' and a 10\% probability of choosing to `Move'. If a single update drastically shifts these probabilities to 10\% `Chop' and 90\% `Move', the KL divergence between the two policies will spike. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:kl_divergence}, a massive spike indicates that the optimizer has aggressively destroyed the previously learned behavior, potentially causing the agent to forget fundamental skills.

To prevent this, PPO implementations often calculate an approximation of the KL divergence during the training loop. If this calculated divergence crosses a strict, predefined threshold (the Target KL, typically around $0.01$ to $0.015$), the optimizer immediately aborts any remaining training epochs for that batch of data~\parencite{Schulman_Wolski_Dhariwal_Radford_Klimov_2017}. This \emph{early stopping} acts as an emergency brake, ensuring that even if an extraordinarily large Advantage signal slips past the clipping mechanism, the policy is physically blocked from mutating too violently in a single step.

\subsection{Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:marl_foundations}

Imagine our wood-collecting agent is no longer alone. We now introduce a second agent into the grid, tasked with mining stone. Suddenly, the fundamental assumption of our standard MDP (that the environment only changes when \emph{our} agent acts) is shattered. Because both agents update their policies and take actions concurrently, the environment becomes a moving target. If the lumberjack moves toward a tree, but the miner accidentally blocks the path a millisecond prior, the environment's transition dynamics become non-stationary from the local perspective of any individual agent~\parencite{Albrecht_Christianos_Schäfer_2024}.

\subsubsection{Decentralized Partially Observable MDPs (Dec-POMDPs)}
\label{subsec:dec_pomdp}

Furthermore, in complex multi-agent domains, an individual agent rarely possesses a god's-eye view of the entire grid. They suffer from local sensory limitations, perceiving only a cropped window of their immediate surroundings. 

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:dec_pomdp}, this scenario is formally modeled as a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP)~\parencite{Oliehoek_Amato_2016}. The diagram demonstrates how the true global environment state is hidden behind an "observation emission filter," essentially creating a fog of war that forces each agent to act strictly on fragmented, local information.

To formalize this mathematically, we extend our previous MDP into the Dec-POMDP tuple $\langle \mathcal{N}, \mathcal{S}, \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \boldsymbol{\Omega}, \mathcal{O}, \gamma \rangle$. We can map these variables directly to our cooperative scenario:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{N} = \{1, \dots, N\}$ is the roster of active agents (e.g., Agent 1 Lumberjack, Agent 2 Miner).
    \item $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}} = \mathcal{A}_1 \times \dots \times \mathcal{A}_N$ is the joint action space. At timestep $t$, agents simultaneously execute a joint action $\mathbf{a}_t = (a_t^{(1)}, \dots, a_t^{(N)}) \in \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}$.
    \item $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \Omega_1 \times \dots \times \Omega_N$ is the joint observation space, representing the limited windows of information each agent receives.
    \item $\mathcal{O}: \mathcal{S} \times \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}} \times \boldsymbol{\Omega} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is the observation probability distribution $\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{o} \mid s', \mathbf{a})$. This acts as the mathematical "filter" shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dec_pomdp}, determining what slice of the true state each agent is permitted to see.
    \item $\mathcal{R}: \mathcal{S} \times \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the globally shared reward signal. If the agents successfully cooperate to build a tool, this single scalar reward is emitted to all agents equally.
\end{itemize}

This structure introduces a critical mathematical consequence. Because an individual agent $i$ cannot perceive the full state $s_t$, a single observation $o_t^{(i)} \in \Omega_i$ instantly breaks the Markov property we established in Section 2.1. The agent cannot rely solely on the present moment, because its present observation is incomplete. 

To restore temporal context, agent $i$ must rely on its memory. It maintains an action-observation history $\tau_t^{(i)} = (o_1^{(i)}, a_1^{(i)}, \dots, o_t^{(i)})$, and parameterizes its decentralized policy as $\pi_i(a_i \mid \tau_i)$. In modern deep learning, this memory is typically implemented using recurrent neural network layers, such as GRUs or LSTMs, allowing the agent to stitch together a continuous understanding of the world from fragmented snapshots~\parencite{Hausknecht_Stone_2015}.

CTDE applies a dual-perspective philosophy to our grid-world. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ctde_mappo}, during the offline training phase, the algorithm acts as an omniscient supervisor. It utilizes a Centralized Critic that has privileged, god's-eye access to the global environment state $\mathbf{s}_t$. Because it knows the exact coordinates of both the lumberjack and the miner, it can accurately judge whether a joint action was truly beneficial. 

However, during online deployment, this centralized structure is severed. The Critic is discarded, and the decentralized Actors are deployed to the grid. They must execute their policies independently, relying solely on their fragmented local action-observation histories ($\tau_i$) without knowing the exact status of their partner.

\subsubsection{Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)}
\label{subsec:mappo}

Multi-Agent PPO (MAPPO) brings our grid-world to life by extending the single-agent PPO architecture directly into this CTDE paradigm~\parencite{Yu_Velu_Vinitsky_Gao_Wang_Bayen_Wu_2022}. The elegance of MAPPO lies in its simplicity: the decentralized Actors remain mathematically identical to standard PPO, while only the Centralized Critic is modified to process the global environment state.

The Centralized Critic Network (Value), parameterized by $\phi$, learns the value function $V_\phi(\mathbf{s}_t)$. Crucially, $\mathbf{s}_t$ is the unclipped global joint state. In our RPG scenario, while the lumberjack only sees the trees immediately in front of them, the Critic sees the entire map, knowing exactly where both the lumberjack and the miner are located, and what resources they hold. By estimating baseline returns using this true reality of the board, the Critic stabilizes the variance in its Advantage calculations. It is optimized by minimizing the Mean Squared Error against the actual calculated returns $\hat{R}_t$~\parencite{Yu_Velu_Vinitsky_Gao_Wang_Bayen_Wu_2022}:
\begin{equation}
    L^{\text{VF}}(\phi) = \frac{1}{B \cdot N} \sum_{i=1}^B \sum_{k=1}^N \left( V_\phi(\mathbf{s}_{t,k}) - \hat{R}_{t,k} \right)^2
\end{equation}
where $N$ represents the number of agents, and $B$ represents the batch size (the number of environment transitions sampled during a training update). This equation simply averages the prediction error across all agents and all sampled experiences.

Simultaneously, the decentralized Actors (the lumberjack and the miner) learn their respective policies $\pi_{\theta_i}(a_i \mid \tau_i)$. Because they operate in the fog of war, their inputs are restricted strictly to their localized action-observation histories ($\tau_i$). Each Actor updates its weights using the exact same clipped surrogate objective from Equation~\ref{eq:ppo_clip}. 

As mapped out in our visual architecture, the gradient flow is strictly separated: the error from the global state flows backward exclusively to update the Critic's weights ($\phi$), while the Advantage signal computed by the Critic is passed outward to the Actors. The lumberjack and miner use this Advantage to update their own neural weights ($\theta_i$). This distinct separation of information allows MAPPO to achieve state-of-the-art sample efficiency across complex cooperative benchmarks~\parencite{Yu_Velu_Vinitsky_Gao_Wang_Bayen_Wu_2022}.

\subsection{Curriculum Learning}
\label{sec:curriculum_learning}

When environments suffer from extremely sparse rewards, an alternative to altering the agent's internal architecture is to alter the environment itself. \textcite{Bengio_Louradour_Collobert_Weston_2009} formalized this concept as \emph{Curriculum Learning}, drawing inspiration from human education. Rather than training a model on the most difficult data (or the most complex RL environment) from the start, the agent is first exposed to simplified, easily solvable versions of the task. 

As the agent masters these basic concepts, the environment's complexity is progressively increased. In reinforcement learning, this typically means a human designer must manually craft a sequence of environments---for example, starting the agent in a tiny grid with the target one step away, and gradually expanding the grid size and adding obstacles. While highly effective at bootstrapping learning, traditional curriculum learning suffers from scalability issues, as it requires extensive domain expertise and manual engineering to carefully design the difficulty schedule for each specific problem.

\subsection{Temporal Abstraction and Reward Shaping}
\label{sec:abstraction_shaping}

While MAPPO exhibits strong sample efficiency in dense tasks, it struggles catastrophically in long-horizon domains gated by sparse rewards. If our lumberjack and miner only receive a shared $+10$ reward when the final enemy is defeated, the probability of them randomly discovering the exact sequence of chopping wood, mining stone, and crafting weapons approaches zero. To mitigate this exploration bottleneck, structural abstraction techniques are required.

\subsubsection{Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS)}
\label{subsec:pbrs}

Reward shaping attempts to solve this by supplementing the sparse environmental reward $r^{\text{env}}$ with an auxiliary dense signal $F(s, s')$. 
However, unconstrained reward additions frequently alter the underlying MDP, causing agents to discover local optima or engage in \emph{reward hacking} (e.g., farming points without completing the actual objective).

To prevent policy distortion, Ng et al. (1999) proved that a shaping signal $F(s, s')$ preserves the optimal policy set $\pi^*$ if and only if it is expressed as the difference of a state potential function $\Phi(s)$~\parencite{Ng_Harada_Russell_1999}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pbrs_canonical}
    F(s, s') = \gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s)
\end{equation}
where $\Phi: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ maps any state to a scalar value.
In our grid-world, think of $\Phi(s)$ as a hot-and-cold compass pointing the lumberjack toward the nearest tree. Adding $F(s, s')$ to the environment reward creates an augmented signal $r^{\text{total}}_t = r_t^{\text{env}} + F(s_t, s_{t+1})$.

As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pbrs_loop}, the discount factor $\gamma$ is mathematically vital. It acts as a conservative vector field, ensuring that the agent is penalized for stepping backward more than it is rewarded for stepping forward. Because $\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t F(s_t, s_{t+1}) = -\Phi(s_0)$, this structural formulation guarantees policy invariance while providing dense guidance~\parencite{Ng_Harada_Russell_1999}.

\subsubsection{The Options Framework for Hierarchical RL}
\label{subsec:options_framework}

Rather than modifying the reward landscape, Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) achieves temporal abstraction by organizing low-level actions into high-level macro-structures. The \emph{Options Framework} formalizes these macro-actions over MDPs~\parencite{Sutton_Precup_Singh_1999}. Instead of deciding whether to step north or south, our agent can select a discrete Option $o \in \mathcal{O}$, such as \texttt{COLLECT\_WOOD}. 

This Option is defined by the triple $\langle \mathcal{I}_o, \pi_o, \beta_o \rangle$~\parencite{Sutton_Precup_Singh_1999}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{I}_o \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ is the initiation set. The \texttt{COLLECT\_WOOD} Option can only be initiated if the lumberjack does not already have wood in its inventory.
    \item $\pi_o(a \mid s)$ is the intra-option policy governing primitive execution while the option is active. This is the low-level MAPPO policy actually steering the agent to the tree and executing the 'Chop' command.
    \item $\beta_o: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is the stochastic termination condition, giving the probability $\beta_o(s)$ that option $o$ terminates in state $s$. For the lumberjack, this triggers at 100\% probability the moment the wood enters the inventory.
\end{itemize}

When an option $o$ is selected, the intra-option policy $\pi_o$ controls primitive actions until the termination condition $\beta_o$ triggers, transforming the underlying decision process into a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) operating over variable temporal steps~\parencite{Sutton_Precup_Singh_1999}.

Crucially, by abstracting the kinetic physics of the grid into discrete semantic tasks, the Options framework sets the stage for a high-level cognitive controller. As explored in the next chapter, this architecture allows Large Language Models to act as strategic orchestrators, entirely bypassing the random exploration bottleneck.
