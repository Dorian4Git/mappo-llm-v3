\section{The Semantic Engine: Large Language Models}
\label{ch:background_llm}

In the previous chapter, we established how the Options framework allows our lumberjack and miner to execute discrete macro-actions, such as \texttt{COLLECT\_WOOD} or \texttt{BUILD\_BRIDGE}. However, MARL agents cannot inherently deduce the logical sequence of these macro-actions. This chapter introduces the theoretical foundations of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their optimization mechanisms. It establishes how these autoregressive models can act as a high-level "Commander," parsing the semantic state of the grid-world and issuing the correct strategic sequence to the agents below.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Foundations of Causal Language Modeling (CLM)}
\label{sec:llm_foundations}

To serve as a Meta-Controller in a reinforcement learning environment, a model must be able to process a current state and make a sequential decision. This makes \emph{Causal Language Models} (CLMs) the ideal architecture~\parencite{Radford_Narasimhan_Salimans_Sutskever_2018}. 

Unlike standard RL policies that output continuous spatial coordinates, CLMs operate over massive discrete vocabularies. They generate text autoregressively, meaning they predict the next logical token (word or sub-word) based strictly on the sequence of past tokens. This step-by-step generation perfectly mirrors the step-by-step decision-making process of an RL agent. 

Modern CLMs, including the Qwen architecture utilized in this thesis~\parencite{Yang_Li_Yang_Zhang_Hui_Zheng_Yu_Gao_Huang_Lv_et_al._2025}, rely entirely on the Transformer architecture~\parencite{Vaswani_Shazeer_Parmar_Uszkoreit_Jones_Gomez_Kaiser_Polosukhin_2017}. To understand this conceptually, imagine our Commander receives a text context: \emph{"Agent has Wood and Stone, craft a..."} As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:clm_autoregressive}, to accurately predict the next target token ("Pickaxe"), the network must understand the relationship between "Wood", "Stone", and "craft" despite the distance between these words in the sentence. Transformers achieve this by dispensing with temporal recurrence in favor of parallelizable \emph{self-attention} mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Scaled Dot-Product Attention and Causal Masking}

The core computational engine that allows the model to connect these distant concepts is Scaled Dot-Product Attention. For a given input sequence, the model embeds the tokens into three distinct weight matrices: Queries ($Q$), Keys ($K$), and Values ($V$)~\parencite{Vaswani_Shazeer_Parmar_Uszkoreit_Jones_Gomez_Kaiser_Polosukhin_2017}. 
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Query ($Q$):} What the current token is looking for (e.g., the word "craft" sends a query searching for materials).
    \item \textbf{Key ($K$):} What previous tokens contain (e.g., "Wood" and "Stone" hold keys identifying them as available materials).
    \item \textbf{Value ($V$):} The actual semantic content extracted if the Query and Key match.
\end{itemize}

The attention mechanism computes a weighted sum of these values, where the weight is determined by how well the query and the key match via a dot-product:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:self_attention}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
\end{equation}

There are two critical mathematical safeguards in this equation. First is the scaling factor, $\sqrt{d_k}$ (the square root of the key vector dimension). If $Q$ and $K$ are large, their dot product grows massive. Feeding massive numbers into a softmax function causes it to saturate, snapping its outputs to absolute 1s and 0s. This flattens the mathematical slope (the gradient) to zero, halting the network's ability to learn, a phenomenon known as the vanishing gradient problem. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ cools these values down, preserving the gradient.

Second, because we are using a \emph{Causal} Language Model, the network must not be allowed to cheat by looking into the future. During training, the entire text sequence is fed into the Transformer simultaneously for parallel processing efficiency. In a standard full-attention mechanism, the $Q K^T$ matrix computes the dot-product relationship between every token and every other token all at once. If our sequence is "Agent has Wood and Stone", the first token "Agent" would mathematically be able to query the future token "Wood" to help predict the second token "has". This would give the model an impossible advantage during training that it would not have during real-time generation.

To prevent this information leak, a strict lower-triangular mask is applied to the $Q K^T$ matrix before the softmax function. This mask overrides the raw attention scores of all future tokens with negative infinity ($-\infty$). When the softmax function is applied, the exponential function $e^{-\infty}$ evaluates to exactly zero, completely severing the connection to future words. 

Visually, for a sequence of 4 tokens, we can isolate the intermediate weight matrix $W$ from Equation~\ref{eq:self_attention} (specifically, the $\text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$ component) to see how the causal mask operates before the final multiplication by the Values ($V$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:causal_mask}
    W = \text{softmax}\left( \begin{bmatrix}
    s_{1,1} & -\infty & -\infty & -\infty \\
    s_{2,1} & s_{2,2} & -\infty & -\infty \\
    s_{3,1} & s_{3,2} & s_{3,3} & -\infty \\
    s_{4,1} & s_{4,2} & s_{4,3} & s_{4,4} \\
    \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix}
    w_{1,1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    w_{2,1} & w_{2,2} & 0 & 0 \\
    w_{3,1} & w_{3,2} & w_{3,3} & 0 \\
    w_{4,1} & w_{4,2} & w_{4,3} & w_{4,4} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $s_{i,j}$ represents the raw, scaled dot-product score between query $i$ and key $j$ (an element of the $\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ matrix), and $w_{i,j}$ represents the final valid probability weight. As shown in Equation~\ref{eq:causal_mask}, when the model is processing token 3 (row 3), it can only attend to past and current tokens 1, 2, and 3. The connection to the future token 4 is mathematically erased ($0$). This resulting weight matrix $W$ is then multiplied by the Value matrix $V$ to produce the final output of the attention mechanism.

\subsubsection{The Autoregressive Loop and Optimization}

The \emph{softmax} function converts the raw attention scores into a valid probability distribution. At the very end of the network, a final softmax layer converts the model's output into the probability $P(y_i)$ of the next token over the entire vocabulary.

The generation process behaves like a strict deterministic time-loop: the model predicts a single word, and that new word instantly becomes part of the unchangeable past context used to predict the very next word (as shown by the blue feedback loop in Figure~\ref{fig:clm_autoregressive}). It is relevant to see how it learns at first when it is trained even so we won't train from a moel from scratch in our implementation. Given a context sequence $X$ and a target sequence of tokens $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_N)$, the network is optimized by minimizing the negative log-likelihood (Categorical Cross-Entropy) over the sequence~\parencite{Radford_Narasimhan_Salimans_Sutskever_2018}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clm_loss}
    L_{\text{CLM}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(y_i \mid y_{<i}, X; \Theta)
\end{equation}
where $\Theta$ represents the parameters of the neural network. As visualized by the backward gradient flow in Figure~\ref{fig:clm_autoregressive}, it penalizes the model when the probability assigned to the correct target token $y^*_i$ is too low, and gradients ($\nabla_\Theta$) are backpropagated to update the model weights.

This foundational pre-training over trillions of words endows LLMs with vast latent world models, encompassing logical dependencies, domain knowledge, and common-sense heuristics. 

But why is this specific architecture so critical for our multi-agent framework? During a standard MARL episode, the local Qwen model acts as a frozen, inference-only "Commander". It is \emph{not} actively learning or updating its weights via Equation~\ref{eq:clm_loss}. Instead, we rely entirely on the Attention Mechanism (Equation~\ref{eq:self_attention}) to perform \emph{in-context} reasoning. When we feed the model the full game state as a prompt (e.g., \emph{"Agent 1 is at [2,3] and holds Wood and Stone. It must craft a Pickaxe,"}), the attention mechanism is exactly what allows the network to mathematically link the available inventory (\emph{"Wood"}, \emph{"Stone"}) with the goal (\emph{"Pickaxe"}) to deduce the correct next macro-action. The autoregressive generation loop then allows it to construct and output the command \texttt{GO\_TO\_WORKBENCH} step-by-step. 

However, as we will explore in the following section, relying purely on a base model's general knowledge is often insufficient for formatting precise JSON commands. We must first adapt this foundation using parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) prior to deployment.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (QLoRA)}
\label{sec:qlora}

While a base LLM understands general logic, it does not inherently know how to act as a programmatic controller. In our MARL environment, the Python simulation cannot parse a conversational paragraph; it requires the Commander's output to be a rigidly formatted JSON object. This structured format allows the environment to reliably sample and extract specific data points, such as priority weights for the Mixing Board, or categorical string options like \texttt{CRAFT\_PICKAXE} for the Meta-Controller. 

Teaching a base model these specific formatting rules via full parameter fine-tuning requires massive GPU clusters, rendering it infeasible for local execution alongside a MARL training loop. To mitigate this, this thesis utilizes Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA)~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. Conceptually, imagine the base LLM is a massive, immutable public library of knowledge. Rather than rewriting every book in the library, QLoRA simply slips a highly specialized, lightweight guidebook into the building that tells the model exactly how to format its thoughts into JSON for our grid-world.

\subsubsection{Quantization and Low-Rank Matrices}

Mathematically, this memory saving is achieved in two steps. First, QLoRA freezes the model's pre-trained weight matrix, $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, using 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023, Hu_Shen_Wallis_Allen-Zhu_Li_Wang_Wang_Chen_2021}. To understand quantization, imagine converting a high-resolution, 32-bit digital photograph into an 8-bit retro pixel image. While you lose microscopic precision, the overall shape and meaning of the image remain perfectly clear, and the file size shrinks drastically. NF4 quantization applies this exact compression to the neural weights, drastically reducing the VRAM required to hold the model's foundational knowledge in memory.

Second, because $W_0$ is now frozen and cannot be updated, we must inject trainable parameters. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lora_architecture}, LoRA injects two small, low-rank adapter matrices alongside the frozen base: matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ and matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$~\parencite{Hu_Shen_Wallis_Allen-Zhu_Li_Wang_Wang_Chen_2021, Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. 

To visualize why this is so efficient, consider a theoretical weight matrix $W_0$ with dimensions $10,000 \times 10,000$. Updating this full matrix would require learning 100,000,000 parameters. Instead, LoRA introduces a "bottleneck" defined by the rank $r$. If we choose a tiny rank, such as $r=8$, matrix $A$ becomes $8 \times 10,000$ (80,000 parameters) and matrix $B$ becomes $10,000 \times 8$ (80,000 parameters). By multiplying them together ($B \times A$), we reconstruct a matrix $\Delta W$ that is still $10,000 \times 10,000$, but we only had to train 160,000 actual parameters (a reduction of over 99.8\%). Equation~\ref{eq:lora_matrices} illustrates this bottleneck visually for a small $4 \times 4$ matrix with rank $r=1$, where training only 8 parameters is required to reconstruct all 16 weights:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lora_matrices}
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ b_4
    \end{bmatrix}}_{B \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 1}}
    \times
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & a_4
    \end{bmatrix}}_{A \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 4}}
    =
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    b_1 a_1 & b_1 a_2 & b_1 a_3 & b_1 a_4 \\
    b_2 a_1 & b_2 a_2 & b_2 a_3 & b_2 a_4 \\
    b_3 a_1 & b_3 a_2 & b_3 a_3 & b_3 a_4 \\
    b_4 a_1 & b_4 a_2 & b_4 a_3 & b_4 a_4 \\
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The Forward Pass and Backpropagation}

To ensure that these untrained adapters do not inject random noise into the network at the beginning of training, matrix $A$ is initialized with random Gaussian values, while matrix $B$ is initialized as a strict zero matrix~\parencite{Hu_Shen_Wallis_Allen-Zhu_Li_Wang_Wang_Chen_2021}. As a result, their initial product $BA = 0$. 

During the forward pass (when the model is generating text), the input $x$ flows through both the frozen base model and the new adapters. The modified forward pass is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lora_forward}
    h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} (B A) x
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a constant scaling factor~\parencite{Hu_Shen_Wallis_Allen-Zhu_Li_Wang_Wang_Chen_2021, Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. Conceptually, $\alpha$ acts as a volume knob. It determines how "loudly" the new specialized JSON-formatting knowledge ($BA$) should be blended into the general world knowledge ($W_0$). 

Once this forward pass is complete and the model generates its sequence, how does it actually learn to fix its mistakes? This is where the Causal Language Modeling loss ($L_{\text{CLM}}$) from Equation~\ref{eq:clm_loss} returns to the stage~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. If the model outputs a malformed JSON, the $L_{\text{CLM}}$ calculates the mathematical error between the model's output and the true desired output. 

The optimizer then uses \emph{backpropagation} to send this error signal backward through the network to update the weights. As visualized by the localized gradient annotations in Figure~\ref{fig:lora_architecture}, the computational brilliance of QLoRA is realized exclusively during this backward pass~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. When the gradients (the update instructions, $\nabla_{W_0}$) reach the massive $W_0$ matrix, they are simply discarded, because $W_0$ is frozen~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. 

Instead, the error signal flows down the new adapter pathway via the Chain Rule of calculus. Let $L$ represent the loss and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial h}$ represent the error signal arriving from the network's output. Because the forward pass computes $B(Ax)$, the backward pass encounters matrix $B$ first. As shown in Equation~\ref{eq:lora_backprop}, calculating the gradient for $B$ ($\nabla_B$) relies on the cached forward outputs of $A$. To calculate the gradient for $A$ ($\nabla_A$), the error signal must flow backward through the transpose of $B$ ($B^T$):
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lora_backprop}
    \nabla_B &= \frac{\alpha}{r} \frac{\partial L}{\partial h} (Ax)^T \nonumber \\
    \nabla_A &= \frac{\alpha}{r} B^T \frac{\partial L}{\partial h} x^T
\end{align}
This mathematical sequence proves why $\nabla_B$ can be calculated immediately using the forward activations, while calculating $\nabla_A$ explicitly requires the error signal to pass through $B^T$. 

Understanding this localized gradient flow is critical to the feasibility of this thesis. In standard RL, agents are relatively small neural networks. However, if our training loop had to calculate and store the gradient $\nabla_{W_0}$ for all 7 billion parameters of the Qwen LLM, the workstation would immediately crash due to an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) error. By mathematically restricting the backpropagation updates exclusively to $\nabla_A$ and $\nabla_B$, we avoid allocating these massive gradient tensors. The optimizer selectively applies these lightweight updates \emph{only} to the low-dimensional adapter matrices~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. 

Combined, this targeted gradient flow and aggressive 4-bit quantization reduce overall VRAM consumption by up to 75\% compared to full fine-tuning~\parencite{Dettmers_Pagnoni_Holtzman_Zettlemoyer_2023}. This ultimate computational efficiency is precisely what enables our Qwen Commander to learn its JSON-formatting rules locally, running seamlessly alongside the heavy MAPPO environment without exhausting the workstation's memory budget.

\subsection{The Qwen Architecture and Model Selection}
\label{sec:qwen_architecture}

To serve as the cognitive controller in a MARL system, the chosen foundation model must possess spatial reasoning and reliable instruction-following capabilities. Because the model must run locally alongside the synchronous MAPPO tensor rollouts, parameter size and inference latency become the primary engineering constraints. A model that generates complex semantic plans but requires 30 seconds to output a response will stall the high-frequency reinforcement learning loop.

To identify a suitable Meta-Controller, several modern open-weights architectures were empirically evaluated via the Ollama inference framework. Ollama was selected for its native support of quantized models, which reduces the memory footprint of large language models while maintaining acceptable inference speeds. The candidate pool included models ranging from 2 billion to 27 billion parameters, specifically \texttt{llama3.2:latest} (2B), \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, \texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{gemma2:9b}, and \texttt{gemma3:27b}.

During preliminary testing, models at the extremes of this parameter spectrum proved unsuitable for the synchronous training loop. Lightweight models, such as \texttt{llama3.2} (2B), offered near-instantaneous inference but struggled to reliably respect the JSON schema required by the Python backend, frequently leading to parsing crashes. Conversely, larger models like \texttt{gemma3:27b} exhibited strong logic capabilities and consistent formatting but required approximately 17 GB of VRAM even with quantization. Given the target deployment hardware (a consumer-grade NVIDIA RTX 5070 with 12 GB of VRAM), the 27B model exceeded the physical memory budget entirely, causing immediate out-of-memory errors before the MAPPO environment could even be initialized.

Among the mid-range models (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, \texttt{llama3.1:8b}, and \texttt{gemma2:9b}), differences in instruction following and context processing became apparent. While both LLaMA 3.1 and Gemma 2 fit within the memory envelope and performed adequately on general reasoning tasks, they occasionally hallucinated keys or deviated from the strict JSON hierarchy required for parsing environment commands. In contrast, \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} demonstrated more reliable capabilities for programmatic orchestration. Standard industry benchmarks support this empirical observation: as shown in Table~\ref{tab:model_benchmarks}, Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct outperforms its peers in both zero-shot coding and multi-step reasoning (measured by HumanEval and GSM8K, respectively)\footnote{HumanEval evaluates a model's ability to synthesize functional Python programs, while GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) tests its capacity to solve multi-step logical problems.}~\parencite{Qwen_Yang_Yang_Zhang_Hui_Zheng_Yu_Li_Liu_Huang_et_al_2024}.

At roughly 4.7 GB of VRAM usage, it fits within the hardware constraints without interfering with concurrent PyTorch processes. Qwen 2.5 strikes a practical balance between speed and reasoning: it provides fast, latency-bound token generation while possessing the logical capacity required to navigate task dependency graphs (DAGs). The architecture's extensive pre-training on code and structured data translates well to the deterministic formatting needed for an RL controller. By coupling this efficient base model with QLoRA fine-tuning, the 7B parameters can be locally specialized to act as a reliable, low-latency orchestrator.

\subsection{Neuro-Symbolic Orchestration and Verbal Reflection}
\label{sec:llm_rl_integration}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:abstraction_shaping}, pure deep reinforcement learning struggles with severe exploration bottlenecks in sparse-reward environments. While the Options Framework offers a mathematical solution to temporal abstraction, designing the rules for when to trigger an Option traditionally demands extensive human engineering. 

Because LLMs develop powerful logic and reasoning skills during their initial pre-training, they can be deployed as \emph{zero-shot}\footnote{In machine learning, "zero-shot" refers to a model's ability to successfully perform a task it was not explicitly trained for, relying purely on generalized knowledge acquired during pre-training.} semantic orchestrators. This means the model can manage complex tasks right out of the box, completely bypassing the need for human engineers to write manual, hard-coded decision rules. Frameworks such as ReAct~\parencite{Yao_Zhao_Yu_Du_Shafran_Narasimhan_Cao_2023} have shown that interleaving language reasoning with concrete actions allows models to solve multi-step problems autonomously. In reinforcement learning, systems like EUREKA~\parencite{ma2024eurekahumanlevelrewarddesign} and PRM4RL~\parencite{Zhang_Gao_Jiang_Chen_Pan_Zhang_Deng_2025} successfully use LLMs to automatically write and refine reward functions.

Rather than relying on a single pre-existing framework, this thesis synthesizes these disparate concepts into a unified, dual-tier architecture, building upon the principles of a Neuro-Symbolic Hierarchy~\parencite{Mitchener_2022}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:neuro_symbolic_hierarchy}, the high-level LLM Meta-Controller analyzes the environment and dictates strategic goals (e.g., "Craft a Pickaxe") by reasoning over a semantic Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of task dependencies. These text-based decisions are translated into discrete Options and passed down to the low-level MAPPO execution workers, which handle the high-frequency kinetic control (e.g., navigating the grid and avoiding obstacles).

However, the low-level agents may sometimes fail to achieve the assigned goal. To solve this, language agents can leverage \emph{Verbal Reflection}~\parencite{Shinn_Cassano_Berman_Gopinath_Narasimhan_Yao_2023} to evaluate and correct execution failures. As detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:verbal_reflection}, if the Commander issues a \texttt{MINE\_IRON} option but the miner stagnates and fails to complete it after 150 steps, the environment halts. A reflection prompt containing the failure context is sent back to the LLM. This forces the model to explicitly diagnose its mistake (e.g., realizing it forgot to assign \texttt{CRAFT\_PICKAXE} first) and issue a corrected macro-action.

Together, these assembled mechanisms form a complete neuro-symbolic feedback loop: the LLM dictates the logical \emph{what}, and the MAPPO algorithm learns the spatial \emph{how}.
