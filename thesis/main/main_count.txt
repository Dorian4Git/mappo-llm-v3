Introduction

1.1

Context: Multi-Agent Systems in Open-World Environments

The ability to model, simulate, and optimize the behavior of multiple interacting entities is
a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)
provides a mathematical framework for training decentralized agents to maximize a shared or
competitive utility. As the field has matured, researchers have increasingly utilized Massively
Multiplayer Online Role-Playing Games (MMORPGs) and open-world environments, such as
Neural MMO and Minecraft, as microcosms for real-world complexity. These environments
require agents to navigate vast state spaces, manage resources, and engage in high-level
strategic coordination.
To solve these tasks, the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm
has emerged as the standard architecture. Under this paradigm, algorithms such as MultiAgent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO) have demonstrated surprising effectiveness in
cooperative settings. By utilizing a centralized critic that has access to the global state during
training, agents can learn robust decentralized execution policies that rely solely on local
observations.

1.2 Problem Statement: Sparse Rewards and the Exploration Bottleneck
Despite the success of algorithms like MAPPO, pure deep reinforcement learning struggles
catastrophically in environments characterized by long-horizon, sparse-reward structures. In
complex RPG-style environments, progress is often gated behind strict dependency graphs (e.g.,
agents must jointly collect wood and stone, navigate to a workbench, craft a pickaxe, and then
coordinate to mine iron).
Traditional RL agents rely on random exploration to discover these initial reward signals. However,
as the depth of the dependency chain increases, the probability of agents randomly executing
the exact sequence of required motor commands approaches zero. While engineers traditionally
attempt to solve this via dense, hand-crafted reward shaping, this introduces significant risks.
Poorly designed shaping signals frequently lead to reward hacking, where agents optimize
for surrogate metrics without achieving the true environmental milestone (Ma et al., 2024).
Furthermore, as multiple agents update their policies simultaneously, the environment becomes
highly non-stationary from the perspective of any individual agent, leading to variance spikes
and policy collapse (Lowe et al., 2020). Consequently, the fundamental problem lies in bridging
the gap between high-level semantic planning (knowing what to do) and low-level kinetic control
(learning how to do it) without destabilizing the multi-agent learning process.
To systematically investigate this intersection of large language models and multi-agent reinforcement learning, this thesis poses the following three central research questions:
RQ1: How can the zero-shot semantic reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models be
utilized to dynamically shape potential-based rewards, and to what extent does this

continuous shaping mitigate the exploration bottlenecks inherent in sparse-reward MARL
environments?
RQ2: Does a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning architecture, wherein the LLM functions
as a Meta-Controller issuing discrete macro-actions (Options), provide superior sample
efficiency and coordination compared to continuous dynamic reward shaping when solving
complex, long-horizon dependency graphs?
RQ3: What are the fundamental vulnerabilities of neuro-symbolic MARL architectures regarding
semantic over-confidence and induced over-correction, and how can mathematical constraints (such as Kullback-Leibler divergence guardrails) stabilize the underlying execution
policies against these non-stationary objective shifts?

1.3

Proposed Approach: Neuro-Symbolic MARL

To resolve this exploration bottleneck, this thesis proposes and evaluates two novel neurosymbolic MARL architectures. We treat the learning process as a dual-tier problem, introducing
Large Language Models (LLMs) as high-level semantic reasoners coupled with low-level MAPPO
execution policies (Yu et al., 2022). This approach leverages the pre-trained world knowledge of
LLMs to synergize reasoning and acting (Yao et al., 2023), bypassing the need for the agents to
discover basic logical dependencies via trial-and-error.
Specifically, the subject is addressed through two progressive implementations:
1. Dynamic Reward Shaping (The Mixing Board): An architecture where the LLM acts as a
strategic meta-supervisor. By observing aggregate training statistics, the LLM dynamically
adjusts priority weights over subtasks. These weights govern a continuous Potential-Based
Reward Shaping (PBRS) signal (Ng et al., 1999), guiding the MAPPO agents toward
critical bottlenecks while theoretically preserving the optimal policy.
2. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (The Meta-Controller): A discrete architecture
building upon the Options framework (Sutton; Precup, et al., 1999) and hierarchical metacontrollers (Kulkarni et al., 2016). The LLM completely replaces the traditional symbolic
planner, issuing temporally extended macro-actions directly to the agents. By augmenting
the agents’ observation space with one-hot option encodings and enforcing strict KullbackLeibler (KL) divergence guardrails, this approach enables agents to master the complete
dependency graph and adapt to zero-shot semantic shifts in the environment.
Through these architectures, this work demonstrates how the zero-shot planning capabilities
of quantized, self-hosted language models (QLoRA) (Dettmers; Pagnoni, et al., 2023) can be
seamlessly integrated into deep reinforcement learning pipelines to solve complex cooperative
tasks.

Background MARL

This chapter establishes the theoretical foundations required to contextualize the neuro-symbolic
multi-agent architectures presented in this thesis. We begin by formalizing single-agent Markovian decision processes and policy optimization. We then extend these concepts to multi-agent
environments under partial observability, detailing the Centralized Training with Decentralized
Execution (CTDE) paradigm and Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO). Finally,
we examine temporal abstraction frameworks—specifically Potential-Based Reward Shaping
(PBRS) and the Options framework—before reviewing modern integrations of Large Language
Models (LLMs) as cognitive controllers.

2.1

Fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) formalizes sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where
an autonomous agent learns to optimize its behavior through interaction with a dynamic environment(Sutton; Barto, 2020). Imagine an agent dropped into a complex grid-world, tasked
with navigating a forest to collect wood. At each step, it observes its surroundings, chooses a
direction, and receives a numerical reward if it successfully harvests the resource.

2.1.1

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)

A fully observable single-agent control problem is mathematically formalized as a Markov
Decision Process (MDP) (Szepesvári, 2011). Conceptually, an MDP provides a structured
framework for modeling decision-making in environments where outcomes are partly random
and partly under the control of the agent.
Figure 1 illustrates this foundational interaction loop. At each discrete timestep, the agent
observes the current state of the world, consults its internal strategy (the policy π) to select an
action, and executes it. The environment then computes the consequences, responding with the
subsequent state and a scalar reward signal.

Building on this property, the MDP is formally defined by the 5-tuple ⟨S, A, P, R, γ⟩ (Szepesvári,
2011). We can map each of these mathematical variables directly to our wood-collecting agent:
• S is the set of all valid states. In our example, a state s includes the agent’s (x, y)
coordinates on the grid and whether its inventory currently contains wood.
• A is the set of available actions. For our agent, this might be limited to {Move North, Move
South, Interact/Chop}.
• P : S × A × S → [0, 1] is the transition probability. If the agent chooses the ’Move North’
action, P defines the chance it actually lands on the tile above (e.g., 100%, or perhaps
90% if the environment includes slippery terrain).
• R : S × A → R is the reward function. This is the environment’s feedback, such as emitting
+1.0 for successfully chopping the wood, or −0.01 for wasting a step.
• γ ∈ [0, 1) is the discount factor. This is a mathematical trick (e.g., 0.99) that makes
immediate rewards slightly more valuable than future rewards, forcing the agent to collect
the wood efficiently rather than wandering the grid forever.
To navigate this world, the agent needs a strategy, known as a policy. Rather than a rigid set of
if/then rules, modern RL utilizes a stochastic (probabilistic) policy, denoted mathematically as
π(a | s) = P[At = a | St = s] (Sutton; Barto, 2020). Intuitively, this means that given a specific
state s, the policy outputs a probability distribution over all possible actions. For instance, if the
agent is standing directly next to a tree, its policy π might assign a 95% probability to the ’Chop’
action, and a 5% probability to ’Move South’ to encourage a slight amount of random exploration.
The ultimate goal of the agent is to optimize this policy to maximize its expected discounted

2.1.2

Policy Optimization and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

To train our wood-collecting agent, we must optimize its policy. Instead of memorizing a rigid
table of every possible grid coordinate, modern reinforcement learning parameterizes the policy
using a neural network, denoted as πθ (a | s) with trainable weights θ ∈ Rd (Sutton; Barto, 2020).
This network is commonly referred to as the Actor.
To help the Actor learn, we introduce a second neural network called the Critic, parameterized by
weights ϕ. The Critic learns the Value function, Vϕ (s), which is its estimate of the total expected
return from a given state. Conceptually, this is the agent’s "gut feeling" about a location. If the

agent stands next to a forest, the Critic outputs a high value; if it stands next to a hazard, it
outputs a low value.

2.1.3

The Temporal Difference (TD) Error

Because the Critic’s Value function is a prediction, it requires a mathematical mechanism to
measure when its predictions are wrong. This is achieved via the Temporal Difference Error
(TD-Error), denoted as δt (Sutton; Barto, 2020).
To build intuition, consider our lumberjack agent. Suppose the Critic predicts that from its current
state st , it expects to gather 50 future points (Vϕ (st ) = 50). The agent then executes an action,
immediately receives a small reward of 5 from the environment (rt = 5), and lands in a new state
st+1 . The Critic evaluates this new state and predicts 45 future points (Vϕ (st+1 ) = 45).
The total updated estimate is the immediate reward plus the discounted future prediction:
rt + γVϕ (st+1 ). If we assume γ = 1 for simplicity, the new estimate is 5 + 45 = 50. Because the
new estimate perfectly matches the old prediction (50 = 50), the TD-Error is exactly 0. The Critic
was perfectly accurate.
However, if the agent unexpectedly finds a hidden cache of wood and receives an immediate
reward of 20, the new estimate becomes 20 + 45 = 65. The TD-Error is the gap between reality
and expectation: δt = 65 − 50 = +15. This positive "surprise" signal forces the Critic to update
its weights to make better predictions in the future. Formally, the TD-Error is defined as:
δt = rt + γVϕ (st+1 ) − Vϕ (st )

update frequently causes the network to "fall off a cliff," catastrophically forgetting previously
learned fundamental rules (such as avoiding boundaries).

much one probability distribution diverges from a second, reference probability distribution. In

discrete action spaces, the KL divergence is defined as:

To prevent this, PPO implementations often calculate an approximation of the KL divergence
during the training loop. If this calculated divergence crosses a strict, predefined threshold
(the Target KL, typically around 0.01 to 0.015), the optimizer immediately aborts any remaining
training epochs for that batch of data (Schulman et al., 2017). This early stopping acts as an

clipping mechanism, the policy is physically blocked from mutating too violently in a single step.

2.2

Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Imagine our wood-collecting agent is no longer alone. We now introduce a second agent into
the grid, tasked with mining stone. Suddenly, the fundamental assumption of our standard MDP
(that the environment only changes when our agent acts) is shattered. Because both agents
update their policies and take actions concurrently, the environment becomes a moving target. If
the lumberjack moves toward a tree, but the miner accidentally blocks the path a millisecond
prior, the environment’s transition dynamics become non-stationary from the local perspective of
any individual agent (Albrecht et al., 2024).

view of the entire grid. They suffer from local sensory limitations, perceiving only a cropped

CTDE applies a dual-perspective philosophy to our grid-world. As shown in Figure 6, during the

2.2.3

Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)

Multi-Agent PPO (MAPPO) brings our grid-world to life by extending the single-agent PPO
architecture directly into this CTDE paradigm (Yu et al., 2022). The elegance of MAPPO lies in
its simplicity: the decentralized Actors remain mathematically identical to standard PPO, while

only the Centralized Critic is modified to process the global environment state.
The Centralized Critic Network (Value), parameterized by ϕ, learns the value function Vϕ (st ).
Crucially, st is the unclipped global joint state. In our RPG scenario, while the lumberjack only
sees the trees immediately in front of them, the Critic sees the entire map, knowing exactly where
both the lumberjack and the miner are located, and what resources they hold. By estimating
baseline returns using this true reality of the board, the Critic stabilizes the variance in its
Advantage calculations. It is optimized by minimizing the Mean Squared Error against the actual

cooperative benchmarks (Yu et al., 2022).

2.3

Curriculum Learning

When environments suffer from extremely sparse rewards, an alternative to altering the agent’s
internal architecture is to alter the environment itself. Bengio et al. (2009) formalized this concept
as Curriculum Learning, drawing inspiration from human education. Rather than training a model
on the most difficult data (or the most complex RL environment) from the start, the agent is first
exposed to simplified, easily solvable versions of the task.
As the agent masters these basic concepts, the environment’s complexity is progressively
increased. In reinforcement learning, this typically means a human designer must manually craft
a sequence of environments—for example, starting the agent in a tiny grid with the target one
step away, and gradually expanding the grid size and adding obstacles. While highly effective
at bootstrapping learning, traditional curriculum learning suffers from scalability issues, as it
requires extensive domain expertise and manual engineering to carefully design the difficulty
schedule for each specific problem.

2.4

Temporal Abstraction and Reward Shaping

While MAPPO exhibits strong sample efficiency in dense tasks, it struggles catastrophically in
long-horizon domains gated by sparse rewards. If our lumberjack and miner only receive a shared
+10 reward when the final enemy is defeated, the probability of them randomly discovering the
exact sequence of chopping wood, mining stone, and crafting weapons approaches zero. To
mitigate this exploration bottleneck, structural abstraction techniques are required.

2.4.1

Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS)

Reward shaping attempts to solve this by supplementing the sparse environmental reward renv
with an auxiliary dense signal F (s, s′ ). However, unconstrained reward additions frequently alter
the underlying MDP, causing agents to discover local optima or engage in reward hacking (e.g.,
farming points without completing the actual objective).
To prevent policy distortion, Ng et al. (1999) proved that a shaping signal F (s, s′ ) preserves the
optimal policy set π ∗ if and only if it is expressed as the difference of a state potential function
Φ(s) (Ng et al., 1999):
F (s, s′ ) = γΦ(s′ ) − Φ(s)

where Φ : S → R maps any state to a scalar value. In our grid-world, think of Φ(s) as a
hot-and-cold compass pointing the lumberjack toward the nearest tree. Adding F (s, s′ ) to the
environment reward creates an augmented signal rttotal = rtenv + F (st , st+1 ).

2.4.2

The Options Framework for Hierarchical RL

Rather than modifying the reward landscape, Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL)
achieves temporal abstraction by organizing low-level actions into high-level macro-structures.
The Options Framework formalizes these macro-actions over MDPs (Sutton; Precup, et al.,
1999). Instead of deciding whether to step north or south, our agent can select a discrete Option
o ∈ O, such as COLLECT_WOOD.
This Option is defined by the triple ⟨Io , πo , βo ⟩ (Sutton; Precup, et al., 1999):

• Io ⊆ S is the initiation set. The COLLECT_WOOD Option can only be initiated if the lumberjack
does not already have wood in its inventory.
• πo (a | s) is the intra-option policy governing primitive execution while the option is active.
This is the low-level MAPPO policy actually steering the agent to the tree and executing
the ’Chop’ command.
• βo : S → [0, 1] is the stochastic termination condition, giving the probability βo (s) that
option o terminates in state s. For the lumberjack, this triggers at 100% probability the
moment the wood enters the inventory.
When an option o is selected, the intra-option policy πo controls primitive actions until the
termination condition βo triggers, transforming the underlying decision process into a SemiMarkov Decision Process (SMDP) operating over variable temporal steps (Sutton; Precup, et al.,
1999).
Crucially, by abstracting the kinetic physics of the grid into discrete semantic tasks, the Options
framework sets the stage for a high-level cognitive controller. As explored in the next chapter, this
architecture allows Large Language Models to act as strategic orchestrators, entirely bypassing
the random exploration bottleneck.

Background: Large Language Models

In the previous chapter, we established how the Options framework allows our lumberjack and
miner to execute discrete macro-actions, such as COLLECT_WOOD or BUILD_BRIDGE. However,
MARL agents cannot inherently deduce the logical sequence of these macro-actions. This
chapter introduces the theoretical foundations of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their
optimization mechanisms. It establishes how these autoregressive models can act as a highlevel "Commander," parsing the semantic state of the grid-world and issuing the correct strategic
sequence to the agents below.

3.1

operate over massive discrete vocabularies (often exceeding 100, 000 unique tokens, representing words or sub-words). These models rely entirely on the Transformer architecture, which
dispenses with temporal recurrence in favor of parallelizable self-attention mechanisms (Vaswani
et al., 2017).

to accurately predict the next word ("Pickaxe"), the network must understand the relationship

3.2

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (QLoRA)

While a base LLM understands general logic, it does not inherently know how to output perfectly
formatted JSON commands for our specific MARL environment. Teaching it these specific rules
via full parameter fine-tuning requires massive GPU clusters, rendering it infeasible for local
execution alongside a MARL training loop.
To mitigate this, this thesis utilizes Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) (Dettmers; Pagnoni,
et al., 2023). Conceptually, imagine the base LLM is a massive, immutable public library of
knowledge. Rather than rewriting every book in the library, QLoRA simply slips a highly
specialized, lightweight guidebook into the building that tells the model exactly how to format its
thoughts for our grid-world.

W0 using 4-bit NormalFloat quantization. As illustrated in Figure 9, it then injects two trainable,

3.3

The Qwen Architecture and Agentic Capabilities

To serve as the cognitive controller in a MARL system, the chosen foundation model must
possess advanced coding, reasoning, and instruction-following capabilities. This thesis leverages the Qwen model family, specifically utilizing the Qwen 2.5 7B parameters architecture,
representing state-of-the-art open-weights performance (Yang et al., 2025).
A key innovation in the Qwen architecture is the inherent integration of a “thinking mode”
for complex, multi-step reasoning alongside a non-thinking mode for rapid, context-driven

responses (Yang et al., 2025). In the context of reinforcement learning, this duality is critical.
The Commander model can rapidly parse structured JSON inputs representing the lumberjack’s
inventory, while simultaneously allocating internal computational resources to process multi-step
dependency paths (e.g., verifying that a Pickaxe must be crafted before Iron can be mined). By
coupling the Qwen architecture with QLoRA, the model can be locally fine-tuned to act as a
highly specialized, latency-bound orchestrator.

3.4

Neuro-Symbolic Orchestration and Verbal Reflection

As established in section 2.4, pure deep reinforcement learning struggles with severe exploration
bottlenecks in sparse-reward environments. While the Options Framework offers a mathematical
solution to temporal abstraction, designing the rules for when to trigger an Option traditionally
demands extensive human engineering.
Because LLMs develop powerful logic and reasoning skills during their initial pre-training, they
can be deployed as zero-shot 1 semantic orchestrators. This means the model can manage
complex tasks right out of the box, completely bypassing the need for human engineers to write
manual, hard-coded decision rules. Frameworks such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) have shown
that interleaving language reasoning with concrete actions allows models to solve multi-step
problems autonomously. In reinforcement learning, systems like EUREKA (Ma et al., 2024) and
PRM4RL (Zhang et al., 2026) successfully use LLMs to automatically write and refine reward
functions.
As illustrated in Figure 10, this thesis adopts a strict division of labor known as a Neuro-Symbolic
Hierarchy. The high-level LLM Meta-Controller analyzes the environment and dictates strategic
goals (e.g., "Craft a Pickaxe") by reasoning over a semantic Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of
task dependencies. These text-based decisions are translated into discrete Options and passed
down to the low-level MAPPO execution workers, which handle the high-frequency kinetic control
(e.g., navigating the grid and avoiding obstacles).

The Custom MARL Environment

Before detailing the neuro-symbolic architectures developed in this thesis, it is crucial to establish
the experimental testbed. The intersection of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multi-Agent
Reinforcement Learning (MARL) requires an environment that exhibits both kinetic complexity
(movement, collision avoidance, spatial reasoning) and deep semantic dependencies (long
horizon tasks gated by logical preconditions). To this end, a custom 2D multi-agent grid-world
environment was engineered entirely from scratch.

4.1

Environment Design & Mechanics

The environment operates as a cooperative spatial grid where multiple agents must jointly gather
resources, craft tools, and defeat adversaries to achieve a final objective. Unlike standard
dense-reward environments—where agents receive incremental gradient signals based on
Euclidean distance to a target—this environment utilizes a logical sparse-reward formulation.
The agents receive meaningful extrinsic rewards solely upon the completion of discrete logical
milestones (subtasks).

4.1.1

The Dependency Graph (DAG)

The fundamental exploration challenge of the environment is formulated as a strict Directed
Acyclic Graph (DAG) of dependencies. Agents cannot arbitrarily navigate to the final goal; they
must coordinate to satisfy a rigid sequence of material and infrastructural preconditions. The
required topological task progression is illustrated in Figure 12.

4.1.2

Action and Observation Spaces

The agents are trained under the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)
paradigm. During the decentralized execution phase, each agent perceives a 14-dimensional
observation vector. Within this vector, the agents know exactly two things: where they are (the
absolute (x, y) coordinates of the agents) and what they have (the boolean status of the shared
inventory).
However, they are completely blind to the map layout. The environment strictly enforces the DecPOMDP partial observability (established in Section 2.2.1) by completely hiding the coordinates
of the target zones (resources, workbenches, enemies) from the state vector. Because the true
mathematical state of the environment includes these target locations, hiding them creates a
cognitive "fog of war." This forces the agents to physically explore the spatial grid to locate their
objectives, rather than simply calculating a direct mathematical path to a known coordinate.
The discrete action space consists of five parameters: four cardinal movement directions (Up,

Down, Left, Right) and one semantic “Interact” action. The interaction mechanic is highly contextsensitive; for instance, triggering the action while adjacent to a forest tile chops wood, whereas
executing it at a workbench (contingent upon possessing the requisite inventory) initiates tool
crafting.

Visual Representation

navigate the map. Furthermore, it visualizes the dynamic entity zones critical to the dependency
DAG (e.g., forests for wood harvesting, ore deposits for iron mining, and localized workbenches
for tool crafting). While the core simulation runs headlessly via highly optimized arrays to
maximize transition throughput, this renderer serves as an essential tool for visual debugging

4.2

Architecture & Technology Stack

Engineering a custom MARL environment demands a rigorous assessment of the underlying
technology stack, requiring a delicate balance between visual debugging capabilities and raw,
parallelized simulation throughput.

4.2.1

The Necessity of Array-Native Execution over Game Engines

Initially, robust open-source game engines such as Godot 2 were considered for the simulation
backbone. Godot provides a powerful visual editor, robust collision physics, and a mature
node-based hierarchy, making it an optimal choice for traditional game development.
However, modern on-policy deep reinforcement learning algorithms like MAPPO require simulating tens of millions of environment transitions to converge. Full-stack game engines introduce
massive computational overhead for rendering pipelines, audio processing, and internal state
management. Attempting to spawn hundreds of headless engine instances in parallel quickly
bottlenecks system memory and CPU inter-process communication.
Consequently, the environment was engineered natively in Python utilizing Pygame3 and
NumPy4 . This lower-level approach offers critical architectural advantages for reinforcement
learning:
• Headless Vectorization: The environment logic (kinematics, state transitions, collision
detection) is mathematically decoupled from the rendering loop. During the training loop,
the visual renderer is entirely disabled, allowing a vectorized wrapper to step thousands of
environments simultaneously without graphical overhead.
• Direct Tensor Integration: The underlying grid and inventory states are natively represented as NumPy arrays. This bypasses the expensive serialization and deserialization
bottlenecks typically encountered when passing state matrices between a Python-based
RL training script and an external C++ game engine, mitigating Python’s Global Interpreter
Lock (GIL) limitations during rollout collection.

4.3

Libraries & Infrastructure

The codebase relies on a modern, standardized stack of Python libraries to seamlessly interface
the custom environment with the deep neural networks and the large language models:
• MARL Interface: The environment conforms to the PettingZoo API (Terry et al., 2021),
which has established itself as the canonical standard for multi-agent RL (analogous to
the Gymnasium API for single-agent RL). Utilizing the Parallel API variant ensures the
environment integrates natively with synchronous MAPPO tensor rollouts.
• Deep Learning Framework: PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) serves as the computational
backbone for designing, compiling, and optimizing the MAPPO Actor and Critic networks,
leveraging GPU acceleration for Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) and gradient
descent.
• LLM Integration: To interface with the semantic Meta-Controllers, the Transformers library
by Hugging Face is deployed (Wolf et al., 2020). For the Hierarchical Reinforcement
Learning phase (which necessitates rapid, localized inference), the language models are
quantized using bitsandbytes (QLoRA) (Dettmers; Pagnoni, et al., 2023) and served via
highly optimized inference engines to strictly bound the latency introduced during the

4.4

Hardware and Inference Architecture

A critical bottleneck in neuro-symbolic MARL is the severe latency introduced by the LLM during
the training loop. This thesis relies on two distinct inference paradigms—online cloud APIs and
localized serving—dictated by the constraints of the specific algorithms.
For the Dynamic Reward Shaping architecture, which requires infrequent, highly complex
offline generation, this study leveraged the Gemini 2.5 Flash model hosted via Google AI
Studio5 . However, integrating cloud-based models directly into the high-frequency Hierarchical
Reinforcement Learning (HRL) training loop proved intractable. The MAPPO environment
requires thousands of synchronous environment steps per episode. Utilizing an online model for
the HRL orchestrator generated an excessive volume of requests, rapidly exhausting API rate
limits and introducing network latency that stalled the GPU-bound RL rollouts.
To overcome this, the HRL training loop was executed entirely locally on a consumer-grade
workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 5070 GPU (12GB VRAM). Running a foundation
model locally alongside the PyTorch MAPPO actor-critic tensors within a strict 12GB memory
envelope necessitated specific architectural compromises. Consequently, the Ollama framework6
was deployed to provide highly optimized, low-latency local inference.
The Qwen 2.5 7B architecture was explicitly chosen as the local orchestrator over other openweights alternatives (such as LLaMA 3 or Mistral) for two primary reasons. First, its parameter
scale, when combined with 4-bit quantization, allowed it to fit comfortably within the VRAM
constraints without displacing the MARL experience replay buffers. Second, its inherent dual
architecture—combining a dedicated thinking mode for complex chain-of-thought routing with a
non-thinking mode for rapid spatial reasoning—allowed the agent to process the strict dependency DAG effectively without necessitating a larger, memory-prohibitive parameter count.

LLM Dynamic Implementation

This section details the first integration strategy: LLM-driven dynamic reward shaping, informally
referred to as the Mixing Board approach. Intuitively, this approach separates strategy from
execution. Returning to our established metaphor, the LLM acts as the Commander standing at
a strategic mixing board, adjusting priority dials for various subtasks. Meanwhile, the low-level
PPO agents (the lumberjack and miner) (Yu et al., 2022) act as the kinetic workers, navigating
the physical grid-world to fulfill whichever priority is currently dialed highest.
The Commander does not act directly inside the environment. Instead, it periodically observes
the agents’ progress and adjusts a vector of subtask priority weights. These weights govern two
things: (i) which semantic goal the agents’ internal planner selects, and (ii) the magnitude of the
gravitational pull (potential-based shaping signal) guiding the agents toward that goal.
This paradigm aligns with a growing body of work using LLMs as meta-level reward designers for
RL. For instance, EUREKA (Ma et al., 2024) demonstrated that an LLM can write and iteratively
refine reward functions that exceed human design. Similarly, PRM4RL (Zhang et al., 2026) uses
LLMs to decompose tasks and generate dense shaping rewards. More recently, MEDS (Liu et al.,
2026) introduced memory-enhanced dynamic shaping to penalize redundant behaviors, while
GLARE (Yan et al., 2026) proposed translating trajectory events into temporal logic formulas for
dense reward assignment.
The present work shares this motivation but operates at a much faster, latency-bound granularity.
Rather than writing entirely new reward functions from scratch, our Commander simply adjusts
a vector of priority weights that steer goal selection within a fixed, mathematically safe potentialbased shaping framework.
The exposition of this chapter follows the exact chronological dataflow of the implementation:
1. DAG-Conditional Planning (section 5.2): The system prunes impossible tasks based on
the agents’ current inventory, preventing the Commander from issuing invalid orders.
2. LLM Weight Query (section 5.4): The Commander is queried to assign raw semantic
priorities to the remaining valid subtasks.
3. Softmax Budget Normalisation (section 5.5): The Commander’s raw priorities are
mathematically smoothed into a rigorously bounded probability distribution.
4. Potential-Based Reward Shaping (section 5.6): The normalized budget is injected into
the training loop as a dense, magnetic reward signal to guide the workers.

5.2.1

Greedy Planner and Base Rewards

For each parallel environment, the planner acts as a logical filter. It inspects the agents’ current
shared inventory vector x ∈ Z10 and builds a set of feasible subtasks, denoted as F (i) (x).
For example, Agent 0 (the Lumberjack) might be restricted to targeting {Wood, Workbench,
Bridge, Enemy}, while Agent 1 (the Miner) might target {Stone, Workbench, Iron, Enemy, Gold}.
Crucially, a subtask only enters this feasible set if its DAG preconditions are met; if the agents
have not yet crafted a Pickaxe (xpickaxe = 0), then mining Iron is mathematically masked out of
the options (Albrecht et al., 2024).

5.2.2

Cooperative Coupling Guardrail

Because the environment is fully cooperative, certain tasks require synchronized presence.
If either agent selects a cooperative bottleneck task (such as returning to the Workbench or
building the Bridge), the planner overrides individual greedy selections and magnetically pulls
the partner agent to the same target, ensuring they co-locate to trigger the interaction.

5.2.3

DAG Guardrails

Finally, before the Commander’s weight vector is even allowed to touch the planner, it must
pass through a hard programmatic filter (apply_dag_guardrails). While LLMs are powerful
semantic reasoners, they are prone to hallucinations. If the LLM assigns a high priority to
mining Iron (wiron > 0) when no pickaxe exists, it would inject catastrophic noise into the PPO
value network. This programmatic guardrail acts as an absolute constraint layer, zeroing out
hallucinated weights and overriding the LLM output to maintain mathematical stability.

5.3

the end of each PPO update iteration.

Stage 2: The Z-Score Trigger (The SOS Flare)

Just because production has stalled does not mean the agents need new orders; they might
simply be navigating a physically long path. To confirm they are actually stuck, the system looks
at the Centralized Critic.
As established in section 2.1.3, the TD-Error (Temporal Difference Error) measures the “surprise
gap” of the Critic network—the mathematical difference between its prior prediction and the
newly observed reality. A high mean TD-error is not necessarily alarming; it merely indicates
that the environment has a large reward scale.
However, a spike in TD-error variance is a critical failure metric. It means the Critic is fundamentally confused: it is seeing wildly different returns for the exact same states (e.g., sometimes
observing a massive reward, and sometimes a penalty for identical actions). This variance
spike occurs when agents get stuck at a bottleneck, thrash between uncoordinated goals, or
experience severe non-stationarity (Foerster et al., 2018). Therefore, variance acts as the
mathematical SOS flare of a confused agent. Once the plateau gate is open, the system checks
whether the Critic’s TD-error variance has spiked beyond σ standard deviations from its running

This design leverages the synergy between reasoning and acting (Yao et al., 2023). It intentionally
decouples the LLM from absolute mathematical calibration, allowing it to act purely as a metasupervisor (Sun et al., 2024). The Commander is free to express priority differences via wide
"feelings" of importance (e.g., scoring a critical bottleneck at 8.0 and a completed task at 0.5),
knowing the downstream math will safely normalize this into a stable reward budget.
Furthermore, although the planner ultimately selects a single target goal (eq. (12)), outputting
these continuous logit values is essential for mathematical stability. If we forced the LLM to output
binary 0/1 weights, the planner would instantly snap to a new goal like flipping a light switch.
This violent shift would disrupt the Critic’s value predictions and risk catastrophic forgetting. By
using continuous logits, the system can blend the weights over time, transitioning the agents
smoothly toward the new objective.

policy-invariant gradient F ), the agents can learn to navigate safely.

5.7

Async Communication Architecture

In standard Markov Decision Processes, observation and action delays severely destabilize
training (Katsikopoulos et al., 2003). If the PPO workers (the lumberjack and miner) had to
pause and wait for the Commander’s radio transmission (LLM inference) every time an SOS
flare was fired, the simulation would bottleneck. Querying the LLM takes seconds due to network
latency and inference time. If executed on the main thread, the GPU would sit at 0% utilization,
completely halting the MAPPO training loop.
To resolve this, queries are dispatched through an asynchronous LLMBridge module, which
runs a background daemon worker thread. This asynchronous daemon approach mirrors recent
LLM-agent frameworks like GITM (Zhu et al., 2023), which explicitly decouple the environment
clock to accommodate LLM inference latency.
The asynchronous dataflow executes as follows:
1. Trigger: The Critic trigger fires at the end of a PPO update and constructs the prompt (the
situation report).
2. Dispatch: The prompt is enqueued to a thread-safe queue.Queue.
3. Background Processing: A daemon worker thread dequeues the prompt and calls the
LLM backend (Ollama HTTP API, HuggingFace local, or Gemini API) without interrupting
the main simulation.
4. Continuous Execution: While the LLM is "thinking," the RL loop continues gathering new
trajectories using the old priority weights.
5. Seamless Injection: On completion, the callback parses the JSON, passes it to the
RewardInjector, and stores it as “pending weights.” At the next training update, the
CriticTrigger.on_update_end detects the pending weights, applies the EMA smoothing,
and updates the magnetic pull for the next rollout.
This design guarantees that the high-frequency PPO update is never blocked by network latency.
Crucially, this delay does not destabilize the agents because the Commander is only shifting the
macro-strategy (the reward gravity), rather than controlling their direct micro-actions. The LLM
bridge also maintains a thread-safe response cache to avoid redundant queries for identical
prompts, and utilizes an exponential backoff retry logic (2k seconds for attempt k, up to 3 retries)
to handle API timeouts safely.

5.8

Summary

The Mixing Board approach successfully treats the LLM as a meta-level Commander that
reads aggregate training statistics and outputs strategic priority logits. By routing these priorities
through a cascade of safety layers (DAG guardrails, clipping, softmax budget normalization, EMA
smoothing, and auto-rollback), the system ensures that the LLM’s influence remains bounded
and mathematically stable. Finally, the Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) framework
guarantees that this strategic influence acts as a policy-invariant magnetic pull, guiding the
workers out of exploration bottlenecks without corrupting the foundational reinforcement learning
mechanics.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Architecture

The previous section established the Mixing Board architecture, representing a "soft integration"
of Large Language Models into MARL. In that paradigm, the LLM acted as an environmental
architect, manipulating the gravitational pull of potential-based rewards to gently guide the
agents toward logical milestones. While this elegant separation preserves the underlying Markov
properties, it restricts the LLM to an indirect role: it can only suggest a direction, not enforce a
strategy.
To fully explore the spectrum of neuro-symbolic integration, we must ask: what happens when
we grant the LLM direct semantic authority over the agents’ cognitive state?
This section details the second integration strategy: a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning
(HRL) framework. Here, we transition from soft environmental guidance to hard architectural
integration. The LLM ceases to be a distant architect and becomes a direct Meta-Controller.
Instead of adjusting continuous weights over a fixed greedy planner, the Meta-Controller replaces
the planner entirely. It selects discrete high-level Options (macro-actions) that strictly govern the
behavior of the low-level MAPPO policy.
In our established metaphor, the Commander is no longer just shifting the gravity; it is radioing
explicit, non-negotiable orders directly into the sensory inputs of the lumberjack and miner.
The exposition follows the implementation order:
1. Option Definitions (section 6.2): Defining the discrete macro-actions available to the
Commander.
2. Discrete Option Selection (section 6.3): How the LLM is triggered to assign these explicit
tasks.
3. One-Hot Observation Augmentation (section 6.4): How the Commander’s text-based
orders are mathematically injected into the MAPPO neural network.
4. Bipartite Reward Structure (section 6.5): The dual-reward system required to train the
workers to follow these new explicit orders.
5. Staleness and Counterfactual Reflection (section 6.7): How the Commander diagnoses
its own mistakes when an order fails on the ground.

6.3

Discrete Option Selection by the LLM

Because the Meta-Controller issues explicit, discrete commands, it does not need to continuously
monitor the mathematical variance of the Critic network (unlike the Mixing Board approach).
Instead, the Commander acts reactively, stepping in only when a worker completes a task or
fails to make progress.

6.3.1

Trigger Conditions: Success and Staleness

Option selection is triggered dynamically under two specific conditions, evaluated per-environment:
1. Success: The agent successfully acquired the item (the inventory slot changed), meaning
the current Option has terminated and the agent awaits a new order.
2. Staleness: The active option has been running for more than Tstale = 150 steps without
completion. In MARL, a step t → t+1 represents a physical movement or action in the grid.
If an agent executes 150 low-level kinetic actions without satisfying its macro-objective,
the system assumes the agent is stuck (e.g., trying to mine iron without a pickaxe) and
forces an LLM intervention.
Environments that are already awaiting an LLM response (llm_pending) or are on a brief 50-step
cooldown are safely excluded from triggering new queries.

6.3.3

Batched Queries with Deduplication

Training MARL requires running 128 parallel copies of the grid-world simultaneously. If 40
parallel environments all trigger the LLM at the same time because the lumberjack just collected
wood, querying the Commander 40 separate times for the exact same inventory state would
waste massive computational resources.
To optimize throughput, the system employs a deduplication mechanism. It groups the parallel
environments by their unique inventory vectors, sends only one prompt per unique state to the
LLM, and broadcasts the single response back to all environments sharing that exact state.

6.3.4

Environment Freezing During Pending Queries

A critical challenge in asynchronous LLM-agent frameworks is state drift (latency-induced
non-stationarity) (Katsikopoulos et al., 2003). Querying the Commander takes several seconds.
If the RL environment continues to run while awaiting the radio transmission, the lumberjack
would execute random or stale actions. By the time the Commander’s new Option arrives, the
agent might have wandered to the opposite side of the map, rendering the LLM’s decision
irrelevant to the new physical reality. This problem is well-documented in LLM-agent systems:
for instance, Ghost in the Minecraft (Zhu et al., 2023) relies on pausing the simulation while the
LLM reasons about its next action.
To prevent this drift, any environment awaiting an LLM response is physically frozen.
This ensures that the Commander’s decision is applied to the exact physical state it observed,
maintaining strict temporal alignment between strategy and execution.

One-Hot Observation Augmentation

Once the Commander has selected a discrete Option, that command must be physically
transmitted to the worker. In the MAPPO framework, this is achieved by appending a one-hot
vector directly into the agent’s localized observation space, effectively acting as a “radio receiver”
for the Commander’s orders.

6.4.1

Dynamic Enemy State

Unlike the Mixing Board approach, which used the base BatchCraftingEnvV2 environment, the
HRL architecture operates on a custom extended environment (HRLCraftingEnv) that introduces
a dynamic enemy. In this variant, the enemy is no longer a static zone interaction. Instead,
the enemy tracks the agents’ positions and actively chases any agent that crosses the river,
creating a real-time pursuit challenge. Crucially, the combat system rewards coordination: if
both agents attack the enemy simultaneously, they deal 50 HP of damage per step, compared
to only 10 HP for a solo attack. This 5× coordination multiplier makes the FIGHT_ENEMY option
inherently cooperative.
To give the agents spatial awareness of this dynamic threat, the three dimensions that were
occupied by the goal embedding in the Mixing Board approach (indices 2–4) are repurposed to
carry the enemy’s real-time state: its (x, y) position and its normalized health h/100.

option to pursue) rather than the magnitude of shaping.

6.7

Staleness Detection and Counterfactual Reflection

6.7.1

Staleness Detection

The OptionController acts as a watchdog, tracking the age (number of steps) of each active
option per environment. If an option exceeds Tstale = 150 steps without termination, the
environment is marked as stale and the Commander is re-queried.
Crucially, IDLE options are excluded from staleness triggers, because intentional waiting (e.g.,

the lumberjack waiting while the miner collects stone) is a valid strategy.

6.7.2

Counterfactual Reflection

When a staleness trigger fires, the LLM receives a reflection prompt instead of the standard
assignment prompt. This forces the LLM to reason about why the previous option failed—typically
a missing DAG precondition—before proposing a corrected assignment.
This design addresses the observed failure mode where the LLM repeatedly assigned options
whose preconditions were not met (e.g., MINE_IRON without a pickaxe). The reflection mechanism
leverages the LLM’s zero-shot reasoning to debug its own prior hallucination. This synergizes
reasoning and acting, a paradigm demonstrated by ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), and extends it into
the realm of self-reflection and counterfactual critique (Shinn et al., 2023).

6.8

Episode Lifecycle and Option Reset

When an environment reaches a terminal state (gold mined, game over, or truncation at 800
steps), the OptionController hard-resets that environment to its initial options.
The initial options (COLLECT_WOOD for the lumberjack, COLLECT_STONE for the miner) correspond
to the first layer of the DAG, ensuring agents begin collecting resources immediately upon
episode start.

6.11

QLoRA Fine-Tuning Pipeline

While Section 3.2 established the theoretical foundations of Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation,
this section details the complete practical pipeline used to produce the specialized Commander
adapter deployed throughout the HRL experiments evaluated in Chapter 7. As will be demonstrated, a surprisingly brief fine-tuning process on a modest synthetic dataset is sufficient to
dramatically improve the Meta-Controller’s decision quality.

6.11.1

Oracle Dataset Generation

While the training data was ultimately generated synthetically to ensure exhaustive coverage of
the environment’s crafting DAG, the foundational state distributions were originally seeded by
observations from early, successful exploratory RL trajectories. By grounding the generation
process in these empirically observed states, a deterministic oracle function was developed to
systematically enumerate all meaningful inventory combinations (wood, stone, pickaxe, iron,
sword, armor, bridge, enemy, gold) and apply hardcoded DAG logic to determine the optimal
(agent_0_option, agent_1_option) assignment for each state. This oracle encodes the
optimal policy: for example, “if no pickaxe and wood ≥ 1 and stone ≥ 1, then both agents should
CRAFT_PICKAXE.”
To ensure balanced training signal across all phases of the DAG progression, the raw states
are grouped into six progression phases (early collection, pickaxe crafting, iron mining, gear
crafting, bridge building, and endgame combat). Each phase is upsampled to contribute an
equal share of the final dataset, preventing the model from over-indexing on trivially simple
early-game states.
Two augmentation techniques are applied to improve generalization:
1. Stochastic Oracle Augmentation (SOA): Drawing inspiration from Input Dropout and

state perturbation techniques in robust RL (e.g., Domain Randomization), we introduce
SOA to prevent overfitting to perfect state representations. With a 10% probability, a
randomly selected inventory field is zeroed before querying the oracle. For example, if the
true state is [wood: 1, stone: 1, pickaxe: 0], SOA might perturb it to [wood: 0,
stone: 1, pickaxe: 0]. This teaches the model to produce reasonable, safe outputs
even when the prompt contains slightly degraded or inconsistent state information—a
common occurrence during live RL rollouts where discrete observations may briefly lag
behind true kinetic state transitions.
2. Reflection Augmentation (40% injection rate): A synthetic hallucinated failure is
prepended to the prompt: “You previously chose [Incorrect Option]. It failed after [N]
steps.” The model must then output the correct option despite this misleading prior. This
augmentation was an intentional design decision to teach the Commander to recover from
logical errors. However, as will be revealed in Section 7.3.4, this same training signal
inadvertently caused the model to develop a strong prior that any reported failure implies
a logical mistake, even when the failure was purely kinetic (e.g., the agent simply had not
walked far enough yet). This foreshadows the central “Reflection Contradiction” finding of
the experimental evaluation.
The final raw dataset contains 1,200 balanced examples spanning all DAG phases and agent
status combinations.

6.11.2

ChatML Formatting and Dataset Splits

The raw prompt and completion pairs are converted into Qwen’s native ChatML format (Yang
et al., 2025), structuring each example as a three-turn conversation (system, user, assistant).
The system message contains the Commander’s role description and DAG rules, the user
message provides the current inventory state (and, for reflection examples, the failure context),
and the assistant message contains the expected JSON output.
After a 90/10 train–validation split and a 2× training set duplication (to ensure sufficient gradient
updates given the small dataset), the final corpus comprises 2,160 training examples and 120
validation examples.

described in Section 4.4. The strict memory envelope forced several architectural compromises

A critical engineering detail is the use of a custom DataCollatorForCompletionOnlyLM. During
standard supervised fine-tuning, the causal language modeling loss is computed over all tokens
in the sequence, including the system prompt and user message. This wastes gradient signal
on tokens that the model should never generate at inference time. Our custom collator scans
each tokenized sequence for the ChatML response delimiter (<|im_start|>assistant\n) and
masks all preceding tokens by setting their labels to −100. Consequently, the cross-entropy loss
is computed exclusively over the Commander’s actual decision output (the JSON response),
ensuring that every gradient update directly improves the quality of option selection rather than
memorizing prompt boilerplate.

6.12

Summary

The Meta-Controller approach creates a clean separation of concerns: the Commander reasons
about what to do (strategic option selection via one-hot augmentation), while the MAPPO policy
learns how to do it (low-level navigation and interaction). The one-hot conditioning allows a
single network to exhibit option-dependent behaviour, and the bipartite reward structure ensures
that the policy receives both a consistent dense gradient from intrinsic shaping and a grounding
signal from the true environment rewards.
Furthermore, this architecture effectively acts as an automated curriculum generator (contrasting
with the manual Curriculum Learning discussed in Section 2.3). While traditional curriculum
learning requires a human engineer to laboriously design and schedule environments of increasing difficulty, our LLM Meta-Controller dynamically orchestrates a skill curriculum on the fly,
seamlessly guiding the agents from simple resource gathering to complex combat coordination
within a single, static environment.

Experiments and Results

This chapter presents the experimental setup, the environments used for evaluation, and the
results obtained. We specifically focus on validating the hypothesis that a Meta-Controller LLM
can effectively resolve exploration bottlenecks in a sparse Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
(MARL) environment through dynamic reward shaping, and subsequently, how Hierarchical
Reinforcement Learning (HRL) further improves these capabilities.

7.1

Experimental Setup

To rigorously evaluate the proposed architectures, we compare distinct configurations within our
multi-agent resource-gathering environment, structured into two parts.
For the Dynamic Reward Shaping experiments, we evaluate:
1. Sparse Baseline: A standard MAPPO implementation (Yu et al., 2022) relying purely on
sparse environmental rewards (+1 for completing milestones).
2. LLM Dynamic (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite): The proposed “Mixing Board” architecture,
utilizing Google’s Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite model via API as the Commander to dynamically
shift potential-based reward weights.
3. LLM Dynamic (Local Qwen 2.5 7B): An equivalent dynamic architecture utilizing a
localized, smaller parameter model (Qwen 2.5 7B) to observe the efficacy of smaller,
self-hosted LLMs.
For the Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) experiments, we evaluate three configurations:
1. No-LoRA Base (Qwen 2.5 7B): The un-finetuned base model serving as the HRL MetaController (the Commander).
2. QLoRA with Reflection (Qwen 2.5 7B): A QLoRA fine-tuned model (as detailed in
Section 6.11, the Qwen 2.5 7B model was fine-tuned using a 4-bit NF4 quantization
pipeline on 1,200 synthetic oracle examples) with counterfactual reflection triggered on
option staleness.
3. QLoRA without Reflection (Qwen 2.5 7B): The same fine-tuned model, with the reflection
mechanism disabled.
All runs were executed with identical MAPPO hyperparameters (clipping ϵ = 0.2, GAE7 λ = 0.95,
γ = 0.99, across 128 parallel environments).

7.3

Part 2: Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

While the dynamic shaping architecture successfully guided the lumberjack and miner past initial
bottlenecks (like Iron), it ultimately stalled at the combat phase (Enemy, Gold). Modifying the
continuous reward landscape was insufficient to teach the workers the discrete, long-horizon
macro-actions required for coordinated combat.
This limitation motivated the shift to a full Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) architecture, where the Commander does not merely shape the rewards, but explicitly radios
discrete Options (e.g., “Craft Sword”, “Defeat Enemy”) directly to the lower-level MAPPO policy.

update. Over subsequent updates, the policy absorbs the new objective gradually. This pattern
of rare but controlled disruptions produces the lowest mean TD error (0.048) among the three
runs.

7.3.4

The Reflection Contradiction

The staleness data (Table 8) provides the mechanistic explanation for the delay caused by
reflection. The With-Reflection run dispatches essentially the same number of LLM queries
as the other runs (∼12,800), yet accumulates 3.1× more staleness resets than the No-LoRA
base (88,711 vs. 28,927) and 2.3× more than the No-Reflection run (88,711 vs. 39,137). Each
reflection-triggered query thus produces a worse Option, an Option that expires faster and
triggers yet another staleness event.
The root cause lies in how the instruction-tuning dataset was constructed. During QLoRA
fine-tuning, the dataset synthetically injected counterfactual failures: the training prompt stated
“You previously chose [Incorrect Option]. It failed.” The model then learned to output the correct
Option. Consequently, the Commander associated the reflection trigger with a strong prior: my
previous choice was logically wrong, and I must switch to a different option.
During inference, however, option staleness is frequently triggered not by a logical error by
the Commander, but by kinetic delays on the ground: the lumberjack navigating around the
river terrain, the miner queuing at resource nodes, or simply needing more steps to execute a
fundamentally correct plan. When the orchestrator fires the reflection prompt, the Commander,
conditioned by its training data, assumes it made a reasoning error. It abandons the correct
long-horizon option and switches to an alternative, derailing the workers’ execution.

Part 3: Robustness and Semantic Adaptation

Having established that hard integration (HRL) outperforms soft integration (Dynamic Shaping) in
solving the complete dependency graph, we now return briefly to the Mixing Board architecture.
To fully validate the theoretical claims made in Section 5.5.1 regarding stability, we isolate two
critical variables of the soft integration approach: the numerical stability provided by temporal
smoothing, and the zero-shot semantic adaptation capabilities of the LLM Commander.

7.4.2

Computational, Financial, and Energy Overheads

A critical practical consideration when integrating LLMs tightly into the RL training loop is the
wall-clock time overhead. Because the meta-controller queries the LLM dynamically throughout
the episode, relying on external or cloud-based APIs introduces severe network latency and
rate-limiting bottlenecks.
Analyzing the total execution times for a complete 128-update training run reveals this stark
reality. The Standard HRL Baseline (which uses a traditional static meta-controller without LLM
queries) completed training in 8.30 hours. When deploying an external cloud model (Gemini 2.5
Flash-Lite) for the LLM Dynamic run, the training time ballooned to 12.27 hours—a roughly 48%
increase purely due to API latency, despite querying a highly optimized "lite" model. Using a
heavier state-of-the-art online model (such as GPT-4) was entirely infeasible for our full training
comparisons due to strict API rate limits and prohibitive latency over thousands of calls.
In contrast, our optimized LLM Dynamic (LoRA) implementation, running a locally fine-tuned
Qwen2.5-7B model, completed the identical training run in just 7.78 hours. By keeping inference
entirely local, batching requests, and avoiding network overhead, the LLM-guided approach
achieved execution speeds completely on par with (and in this instance, marginally faster than)
the standard HRL baseline. This proves that dynamic LLM orchestration is not just theoretically
advantageous, but computationally viable when deployed efficiently at the edge.
Beyond pure execution speed, the financial and energy footprints of these architectures are
critical factors for independent researchers. Relying on commercial online APIs for the metacontroller generates a staggering volume of requests; simulating 128 parallel environments over
millions of steps yields millions of input and output tokens per training run. Relying on paid
APIs for this volume of queries would render the approach financially prohibitive. Furthermore,
reinforcement learning is highly hardware-intensive. A standard local workstation drawing
approximately 750W continuously over a 12.27-hour API-bound run—or a 13.5-hour unoptimized
local run—results in substantial electricity consumption, which becomes especially burdensome
when multiplied by dozens of iterations, failed experiments, and hyperparameter sweeps. In
regions with high energy costs such as Switzerland, the ability of the optimized local LoRA
implementation to slash training time down to 7.78 hours provides a compounded benefit: it
completely eliminates API token costs while simultaneously reducing the electrical footprint and

associated utility costs by nearly 40% per run.

7.4.3

Environment Shift: The Paradox of Semantic Over-Confidence

A fundamental critique of using LLMs in MARL is whether they rely on genuine semantic worldmodels or merely overfit to node names. To test this, the environment was silently modified
at Update 500: the ‘Workbench‘ zone was renamed to ‘Furnace‘ in the Commander’s prompt
space. A secondary control run replaced ‘Workbench‘ with a nonsense string, ‘Zylorg‘.
This shift was executed by dynamically injecting an alias mapping into the LLMOrchestrator
mid-training, forcing the Commander to output weights for the alias rather than the canonical
node name, which the orchestrator then reversed and fed to the PPO workers.
The initial hypothesis assumed the Commander would cleanly map "Furnace" to crafting success,
while failing on "Zylorg". However, empirical results revealed a counter-intuitive phenomenon:
semantic over-confidence induced severe reward hacking, while semantic ignorance
acted as a natural regularizer.

Conclusion

This thesis investigated the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) with Multi-Agent
Reinforcement Learning (MARL) to overcome the severe exploration bottlenecks inherent in
sparse-reward environments. By structuring the environment as a strict Directed Acyclic Graph
(DAG) of dependencies, we evaluated two distinct neuro-symbolic architectures: continuous Dynamic Reward Shaping (the “Mixing Board” approach) and discrete Hierarchical Reinforcement
Learning (the “Meta-Controller” approach).
The results demonstrate that while LLMs possess exceptional zero-shot semantic planning
capabilities, acting as high-level Commanders for low-level kinetic workers, integrating them
introduces profound challenges regarding reward stationarity, semantic grounding, and temporal
alignment.

8.1

Critical Assessment of Realizations

Our first major realization was the successful implementation of a dynamic Potential-Based
Reward Shaping (PBRS) framework. We demonstrated that an LLM could actively monitor
MARL agents and shift priority weights to guide exploration, successfully unblocking mid-game
bottlenecks that a standard sparse-reward MAPPO baseline failed to solve. Crucially, we proved
the mathematical necessity of Exponential Moving Average (EMA) smoothing; without it, discrete
jumps in the Commander’s weight assignments induced severe Temporal Difference (TD) error
variance, triggering catastrophic forgetting in the underlying PPO policies.
The transition to a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) architecture represented our
most significant qualitative leap. By allowing the Commander to dictate discrete macro-actions
(Options) rather than merely adjusting the gravitational pull of rewards, the agents mastered the
entire environment DAG, reaching 100% completion on the final milestones. Through ablation,
we established that QLoRA fine-tuning is absolutely critical for sample efficiency, enabling the
agents to reach the final objective approximately 5.1× faster than an un-finetuned base model.
Despite these successes, our experiments revealed critical vulnerabilities in neuro-symbolic
MARL paradigms, highlighting a fundamental misalignment between an LLM’s semantic priors
and kinetic reality. We isolated two major failure modes:
1. The Paradox of Semantic Over-Confidence: We discovered that when an LLM possesses strong zero-shot knowledge of a concept (such as mapping a "Furnace" to crafting),
it confidently warps the shaping signal and inadvertently traps the agents in a local optimum via reward hacking. Conversely, when presented with nonsense strings ("Zylorg"), its
semantic ignorance acted as a natural regularizer, allowing the underlying PPO exploration
mechanics to organically solve the bottleneck.
2. The Reflection Contradiction: Endowing the LLM with counterfactual reflection generated
2.3× more staleness resets and actively delayed convergence. Because the LLM was
fine-tuned on synthetic data where timeouts implied logical failures, it learned to doubt
its own correct reasoning when faced with purely kinetic delays. This resulted in rapid,
contradictory option switching that destabilized the PPO Critic, proving that reactive self-

debugging is highly detrimental if the model cannot distinguish between a logical error and
physical friction.
Finally, the computational overhead of running an LLM in the training loop remains a significant
limitation, severely bounding the scalability of this architecture for high-frequency control tasks
despite the use of quantized, localized models.

8.2

Prospective Vision and Recommendations

The findings of this thesis lay the groundwork for more robust neuro-symbolic architectures,
carrying direct implications for complex coordination problems such as robotic swarm control,
automated supply chain logistics, and advanced game AI. To realize this potential, future
research must fundamentally bridge the gap between semantic logic and temporal delays.
Rather than relying on simple text-based timeout triggers, Meta-Controllers should be equipped
with spatio-temporal memory or multimodal state representations. This would allow the Commander to visually or temporally differentiate between a fundamentally flawed plan and a correct
plan that merely requires the workers to spend more physical time navigating the grid.
Furthermore, the stabilization of non-stationary objectives requires refinement. While our static
KL-divergence guardrail (δ = 0.015) successfully prevented catastrophic forgetting during option
transitions, implementing an adaptive trust region could further stabilize the PPO workers.
Scaling the KL threshold dynamically based on the LLM’s confidence or the specific Option type
offers a more nuanced defense against objective shifts.
Finally, the current architecture evaluates cooperative agents executing a shared Meta-Controller
policy. A highly prospective avenue for future work is the development of decentralized HRL,
where multiple LLMs act as adversarial or dynamically allied faction leaders managing subgroups of agents. Expanding into these complex diplomacy and resource-competition scenarios
would thoroughly test the boundaries of MARL and AI alignment.
Ultimately, this thesis demonstrates that while Large Language Models are profoundly powerful
strategic directors for MARL agents, they must not be treated as infallible oracles. The path
forward lies in designing architectures that safely balance the high-level semantic brilliance of
language models with the uncompromising, low-level kinetic realities of reinforcement learning.
